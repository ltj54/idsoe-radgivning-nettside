DIFFERENTIATED TEACHING

Differentiation and academic progression

Differentiation means adapting the challenges in teaching to different starting points and needs. A useful starting point is finding out what the student already knows and using that knowledge to plan further learning.

It is useful to distinguish between more tasks at the same level and tasks that lead to new understanding. Exploration, greater subject depth and work on more complex questions can give students room to develop further.

For students who learn quickly, differentiation may involve less repetition, a faster pace or more complex and cross-curricular tasks. The right approach depends on the student’s prior knowledge, interests and experience.

Early exploration

In early childhood settings and primary school, curiosity can be supported through stimulating activities, wondering and opportunities to investigate children’s own questions.

Further depth

For older students, project work and in-depth subject study are discussed as opportunities to develop interests and explore a topic more thoroughly.

Acceleration: faster academic progression

Acceleration allows a student to move through subject content more quickly. It may be appropriate when the student has already mastered what is being taught. Subject acceleration can enable lower secondary students to take upper secondary subjects. This provision requires a municipal decision and consent from the student or their parents.

The school should plan further progression, support and social belonging with the student. A faster pace should also leave room for depth. See Udir’s guidance on adaptations and subject acceleration for options and conditions (in Norwegian).

Enrichment: greater depth and complexity

Enrichment broadens or deepens the student’s work. Open questions, comparing explanations and projects across subjects can create opportunities for independent thinking. Students can work on the class topic while encountering greater academic challenges.

In mathematics, one example is finding several solutions and explaining when each works. Another is changing an assumption and exploring how it affects the result. The number of tasks says little about how much new learning they offer.

From curiosity to an independent project

Renzulli and Reis describe three forms of enrichment in the Schoolwide Enrichment Model (SEM). They can support one another and need not follow a fixed sequence.

  • Explore: Encounter new topics and discover interests.
  • Practise: Develop inquiry, critical thinking and communication skills.
  • Investigate: Address a real question and create something for an actual audience.

For example, students might explore how the school grounds could better support pollinating insects. They learn about species, practise observation and evaluate sources. Interested students can then investigate a chosen area and present reasoned proposals to the school. The teacher provides guidance throughout.

An original teaching example inspired by the Renzulli Center’s description of enrichment.

Check prior knowledge and learning strategies

Acceleration and enrichment can be combined. With either approach, teachers need to explore what students have mastered and what still needs practice. Interest and in-depth study can motivate learning, but gaps in prior knowledge do not necessarily close on their own.

Make room for creative questions

Unexpected questions can open up opportunities for exploration. The teacher can help turn an idea into a question that can be investigated: What do you think will happen? How could we test it? What do we need to know to assess the explanation?

In a project about plants, students might investigate how light affects growth. Some could describe observations, while others plan comparisons and discuss uncertainty. A shared topic can offer different academic challenges.

Feedback that identifies the next step

Assessment is more than grades. Dialogue and feedback can offer insight into what the student has mastered, how they work and what an appropriate next challenge would be. Adaptations need to be adjustable along the way.

  • Explore prior knowledge, interests and the student’s experiences of the tasks.
  • Plan a challenge that offers opportunities for new learning.
  • Discuss approaches and what the student finds demanding.
  • Review the experiences and adjust subsequent work.

These points illustrate how the topics can inform reflection on teaching.
