When conversations with school become difficult

Talking with school about a child with high learning potential can be demanding. Many parents feel they have to explain, defend and document their child’s needs—often without feeling fully understood.

A recent study by Ben Artzey and Qadach (2024) shows that parents commonly use three different strategies in these conversations:

  • The Motivator seeks cooperation, shares information and tries to find solutions together with the teacher.
  • The Law Enforcer refers to research, guidelines and regulations when the school does not follow up opportunities or agreements.
  • The Mentor devotes most energy to supporting the child directly, including strengthening independence, coping strategies and learning outside school.

Most parents will probably recognise themselves in all three roles. Which strategy works best often depends on the situation, the child’s needs and how open the school is to dialogue.

Why does it become so emotional?

Parents bring not only knowledge to the meeting, but also concerns, hopes and experiences. When their own child is involved, strong emotions are natural.

The aim does not have to be hiding those emotions, but combining them with clear knowledge of what the child actually needs: less repetition, faster progression, greater academic challenge or more flexible learning opportunities.

Some advice for meeting with school

  • Prepare specific examples of the child’s learning needs.
  • Begin with the idea that you share a common goal: for the child to thrive and develop.
  • Discuss needs rather than labels.
  • Be clear about what you want to try.
  • Summarise agreements in writing after the meeting.
  • Arrange a new meeting to evaluate the measures.
  • Consider bringing a partner, professional or another support person.

Advocating for your child does not have to be a struggle you face alone. When parents, school and professionals work together, it becomes easier to create a learning environment in which the child can develop and thrive.

Reference

Ben Artzey, N., & Qadach, M. (2024). Strategies that parents of gifted children use in tense situations with teachers in mixed-ability classes. Gifted and Talented International, 39(1), 46–59.
