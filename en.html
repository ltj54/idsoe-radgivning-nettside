UNDERSTANDING

Learning potential and school achievement

High learning potential is not always reflected in grades or school achievement.

Children may need greater challenges even when their potential is not clearly expressed in everyday school life. Children and students with high learning potential are different from one another. It is therefore important to understand each individual’s interests, experiences and learning needs.

POTENTIAL AND ACHIEVEMENT

Learning potential and the learning environment

It is useful to distinguish between what a child achieves now and what they may be capable of learning and developing. High grades can make some students’ abilities visible. Other students’ potential is less apparent in assignments, assessments or classroom participation.

Learning potential develops in interaction with the environment. An interest in a subject needs encouragement, appropriate challenges and opportunities to practise. Finding learning easy does not mean that further development happens automatically.

Achievement can also change throughout a student’s school years. Previous success does not tell us everything about what a student needs today. An important question is how teaching responds to their current knowledge, interests and needs.

Strengths in one or more subjects

A child may have particular strengths in mathematics, language or science without being equally advanced in every subject. Look at how the student reasons, asks questions and approaches tasks in each subject. Their overall grades may not capture these differences.

A question for reflection

What can students demonstrate through the tasks we set – and what opportunities do they have to show other aspects of what they know?

RECOGNISING LEARNING NEEDS

Explore what the student knows and how they learn

Assessment should guide teaching. A test can show some of what a student has mastered, while observation, conversations and work over time provide a broader picture.

Let students share interests and ideas

Ask what the student enjoys exploring, which questions occupy them and what they would like to make or investigate. Let them share a piece of work or propose a project. This may reveal interests that everyday tasks do not bring out.

Include parents’ observations

Ask for concrete examples of what the child reads, builds, discusses or learns at home. Consider these alongside classroom observations. Differing accounts offer a starting point for further exploration.

Provide several opportunities to show strengths

Use varied tasks and notice the student’s explanations, questions and approaches. Language, previous learning opportunities and co-occurring difficulties can affect what they demonstrate. Give students several opportunities to show what they know.

What can different assessment methods tell us?

Above-level tasks can provide more information when a regular test is too easy. A high score on an easy test reveals little about the extent of a student’s understanding.

Dynamic assessment also explores how a student learns with support, for example through assessment, guided learning and reassessment. This differs from simply using a harder test.

Standardised tests require appropriate expertise and must be used according to their guidelines. Assessment is not a diagnosis or a guarantee of future development.

Read about identification at NAGC and dynamic assessment at ASHA (ASHA’s example concerns language assessment).

From observation to teaching

Choose an appropriate challenge, agree on support and review both learning and well-being. Use the experience to adjust the approach.

Further reading: Udir on identifying students with high learning potential (in Norwegian).

MOTIVATION AND DIFFERING NEEDS

Motivation and a lack of academic challenge

Boredom, frustration and underachievement can arise when students do not experience appropriate challenges over time. If much of the teaching covers what a student already knows, there may be little room to experience effort, new learning and development.

This gives us reason to examine the learning situation before interpreting limited participation as a lack of interest or willingness. At the same time, children differ. A student’s reaction alone cannot tell us their learning potential or explain why they are struggling.

Children may experience learning, impressions and relationships in different ways. An important starting point is to approach each child with curiosity: what engages them, what feels demanding and what makes learning meaningful? No single personality type describes all students with high learning potential.

When potential is not reflected in achievement

A student may have strong abilities yet show little engagement or achieve low grades. Repetition without new learning and limited experience of demanding tasks are relevant factors to explore. Absence and dropout can have several causes; consider the student’s learning, relationships and wider circumstances together.

Learning how to learn

Students who have long completed tasks with little effort also need opportunities to develop study habits and learning strategies. When work becomes more demanding, they may need support with planning, trying different approaches and accepting that understanding takes time.

An appropriate challenge allows room for mistakes and further effort. The teacher can help the student break down a task, review a strategy and ask for help. Discuss how the student learns, including when the answer does not come immediately.

Look for connections

Does engagement change when the student can pursue an interest in depth, explore a new question or work on a more challenging task?

INCLUSION IN PRACTICE

Academic and social inclusion

Inclusion involves more than a student adapting to existing provision. Teaching also needs to be developed around the student’s needs. Physical presence in a mainstream classroom does not by itself tell us whether the student feels included.

Inclusion also involves contributing academically, experiencing social belonging and being met with understanding. A student may attend lessons without feeling that the tasks are meaningful or that their own perspectives have a place.

Equal worth and different learning needs

Equal worth can be respected through different tasks, approaches and levels of support. When everyone studies the same material at the same pace, some may receive too little challenge and others too little support. A shared classroom community can accommodate different paths to learning.

  • Academically: Can the student learn something new and contribute their knowledge and ideas?
  • Socially: Does the student have someone to work with and share interests with?
  • The student’s experience: Does the student feel seen, listened to and part of the community?

These questions need to be explored with the student. Adults’ assessment of good provision may differ from the student’s experience of it.

Academic level does not describe the whole child

A child working on advanced mathematics may still need support with collaboration, disappointment or starting tasks. Different aspects of development do not necessarily progress at the same pace. Explore each area of need without assigning the child a single overall “mental age”.

Flexible approaches that support belonging

These examples can inform planning with the student. Consider learning needs, relationships and the school’s circumstances when choosing an approach.

Lessons in a subject at a higher level

Learning: The student attends some lessons at a more advanced level.

Belonging: Plan connections with their own class, a welcome in the other group and an adult who follows up.

A guided interest group

Learning: Students investigate a shared question, potentially across year groups.

Belonging: Make room for collaboration and new relationships. Review who can participate and how the group works.

In-depth study within the class

Learning: The student explores more complex questions within the class topic.

Belonging: Connect the work to shared discussions and collaboration so that it does not become an isolated activity.

Agree who will follow up, when to review the approach and how the student’s experience will be heard. Check both academic progress and whether the student feels safe and included.

Udir’s guidance explains conditions for grouping and subject acceleration in Norway. Grouping by academic level is subject to specific restrictions.

SAFETY AND RELATIONSHIPS

A safe and predictable learning environment

Children need both academic challenges and adults who take safety and well-being seriously. This applies to students with different learning needs. High learning potential does not in itself mean that a child has experienced trauma.

Make everyday routines understandable

Clear routines can help students know what will happen and what is expected. Explain the activity, prepare for transitions and give notice of changes. Preventing bullying and exclusion belongs alongside this work: students need to feel safe both in lessons and within the community.

Build trust through everyday interactions

Relationships develop when adults are present, show interest and follow through on what they say. A calm greeting, time to listen and an agreed check-in can be practical ways to show care. Ask what the student needs to make the school day work better.

Understand reactions before choosing support

Distressing and traumatic experiences can affect learning, behaviour and relationships. A trauma-sensitive approach means being aware of this possibility while exploring different explanations. Restlessness, withdrawal or difficulty concentrating do not on their own indicate trauma.

Notice when situations become difficult, what is happening around the student and what helps. Combine clear, respectful boundaries with support. Adaptations should reflect the student’s needs and experiences.

Offer choices and ways to express oneself

Conversation, drawing, writing or practical activities can offer different ways to participate and express oneself. Give the student appropriate choices without requiring them to disclose distressing experiences. Everyday classroom activities should not be used as a method for processing traumatic memories.

Work together on further support

Schools can provide safety, adjustments and ongoing support in everyday life. When difficulties affect a student’s functioning, collaboration with relevant professionals may be needed. Assessing and treating trauma reactions requires qualified healthcare professionals.

Further reading: NCTSN on schools and trauma reactions and suggestions for educators (PDF).

DIFFERENTIATED TEACHING

Differentiation and academic progression

Differentiation means adapting the challenges in teaching to different starting points and needs. A useful starting point is finding out what the student already knows and using that knowledge to plan further learning.

It is useful to distinguish between more tasks at the same level and tasks that lead to new understanding. Exploration, greater subject depth and work on more complex questions can give students room to develop further.

For students who learn quickly, differentiation may involve less repetition, a faster pace or more complex and cross-curricular tasks. The right approach depends on the student’s prior knowledge, interests and experience.

Early exploration

In early childhood settings and primary school, curiosity can be supported through stimulating activities, wondering and opportunities to investigate children’s own questions.

Further depth

For older students, project work and in-depth subject study are discussed as opportunities to develop interests and explore a topic more thoroughly.

Acceleration: faster academic progression

Acceleration allows a student to move through subject content more quickly. It may be appropriate when the student has already mastered what is being taught. Subject acceleration can enable lower secondary students to take upper secondary subjects. This provision requires a municipal decision and consent from the student or their parents.

The school should plan further progression, support and social belonging with the student. A faster pace should also leave room for depth. See Udir’s guidance on adaptations and subject acceleration for options and conditions (in Norwegian).

Enrichment: greater depth and complexity

Enrichment broadens or deepens the student’s work. Open questions, comparing explanations and projects across subjects can create opportunities for independent thinking. Students can work on the class topic while encountering greater academic challenges.

In mathematics, one example is finding several solutions and explaining when each works. Another is changing an assumption and exploring how it affects the result. The number of tasks says little about how much new learning they offer.

From curiosity to an independent project

Renzulli and Reis describe three forms of enrichment in the Schoolwide Enrichment Model (SEM). They can support one another and need not follow a fixed sequence.

  • Explore: Encounter new topics and discover interests.
  • Practise: Develop inquiry, critical thinking and communication skills.
  • Investigate: Address a real question and create something for an actual audience.

For example, students might explore how the school grounds could better support pollinating insects. They learn about species, practise observation and evaluate sources. Interested students can then investigate a chosen area and present reasoned proposals to the school. The teacher provides guidance throughout.

An original teaching example inspired by the Renzulli Center’s description of enrichment.

Check prior knowledge and learning strategies

Acceleration and enrichment can be combined. With either approach, teachers need to explore what students have mastered and what still needs practice. Interest and in-depth study can motivate learning, but gaps in prior knowledge do not necessarily close on their own.

Make room for creative questions

Unexpected questions can open up opportunities for exploration. The teacher can help turn an idea into a question that can be investigated: What do you think will happen? How could we test it? What do we need to know to assess the explanation?

In a project about plants, students might investigate how light affects growth. Some could describe observations, while others plan comparisons and discuss uncertainty. A shared topic can offer different academic challenges.

Feedback that identifies the next step

Assessment is more than grades. Dialogue and feedback can offer insight into what the student has mastered, how they work and what an appropriate next challenge would be. Adaptations need to be adjustable along the way.

  • Explore prior knowledge, interests and the student’s experiences of the tasks.
  • Plan a challenge that offers opportunities for new learning.
  • Discuss approaches and what the student finds demanding.
  • Review the experiences and adjust subsequent work.

These points illustrate how the topics can inform reflection on teaching.

FRIENDSHIP AND COMMUNITY

Friendship and shared interests

Meeting others who share interests, questions or a sense of humour can be valuable. The class community matters, but being the same age does not automatically create shared interests.

Projects across year groups and meeting places centred on academic interests are discussed as opportunities for connection and belonging. The key is to understand where the child experiences positive relationships and can participate actively.

Take the student’s experience of bullying seriously

Ask how the student is doing, where they feel unsafe and what support they need. Good grades alone do not tell us how a child is doing socially. Follow-up should reflect the individual student’s situation.

Depth and shared interests

In-depth study, contact with professional communities and a community of other interested children can support both academic and social development. Such opportunities should be considered alongside the school’s work and the child’s own interests.

Development without pressure to perform

Supporting an interest can be about enjoyment, depth and community. A child’s worth does not depend on their achievements.

THE STUDENT’S VOICE

Student participation and reflection on learning

A recurring point is that children themselves have important knowledge about how school life feels. This applies to learning, relationships and the sense of belonging.

Reflection on learning can provide a more nuanced basis for adapting teaching: when does collaboration work well? When is working alone useful? What helps the student move forward?

Questions that can start a conversation

  • What would you like to learn more about?
  • When do you really need to think hard to make progress?
  • Who do you enjoy exploring and discussing things with?
  • When do you feel your ideas are listened to?
  • What could make your school day more meaningful?

These suggestions support dialogue. They are not quotations or an assessment tool.

ADULTS’ KNOWLEDGE

Professional knowledge and collaboration in schools

Adults’ knowledge and an open, inclusive attitude are important. Students’ questions and alternative ideas can be a starting point for further learning. This requires adults to try to understand what the student is expressing.

School leadership and collaboration among colleagues are discussed as important settings for reviewing practice: how do we find out what students know? What do we do when a student has already mastered the content? How do we monitor whether an adaptation is working?

Knowledge from early childhood education onwards and greater attention to the topic in professional education can strengthen this work. Differentiation belongs within a wider aim of supporting different students and enabling participation in the community.

School leadership and organisation

Adaptations need a shared plan. School leaders can allocate time for collaboration, clarify responsibilities and enable coordination across subjects and year groups. At transitions, information about the student’s learning and previous adaptations should be passed on.

Share teaching resources and develop expertise together

A shared collection of open tasks, advanced material and relevant digital tools can support teachers. Staff can discuss which students the activities suit, what guidance they need and what the experiences show.

Udir’s professional development package on high learning potential is a resource for schools and the educational and psychological counselling service (PPT), available in Norwegian.

Resources and follow-up across schools

School authorities and national government influence opportunities through priorities, professional development and access to resources. A useful goal for school development is to monitor both academic progress and students’ experience of participation and belonging.

A shared understanding of learning needs

A shared vocabulary for learning needs can make it easier to examine experiences, discuss support and listen to both students’ and adults’ perspectives.

QUESTIONS AND ANSWERS

Questions about learning potential

Some central distinctions from the in-depth discussion are brought together here.

Is high learning potential the same as good grades?

No. Grades describe demonstrated achievement in a particular context. It is also important to understand the student’s knowledge, interests, learning environment and opportunities for further learning.

Do students who learn easily also need support?

Learning easily does not mean that a student automatically encounters appropriate challenges. Support may involve deeper study, feedback, academic discussion and opportunities to develop interests with others.

Are more tasks always a useful adaptation?

More tasks do not necessarily lead to new learning if the student has already mastered the content. The starting point should be what they need to learn next and whether tasks allow exploration, greater depth or more complex questions.

How do we know whether a student feels included?

We need to listen to the student’s own experience. Can they contribute academically, find a community and feel understood? Physical presence in the classroom does not give the whole answer.

Does differentiation mean that some children are worth more than others?

No. The issue is differing learning needs, not differences in human worth. Differentiation is about supporting all students and giving them opportunities to develop.

AUDIENCES

Information for parents and professionals

For adults who want to understand and support children and students with high learning potential.

A child’s learning and well-being need to be understood from several perspectives. Parents know everyday life and interests, schools know the teaching and classroom environment, and healthcare professionals can contribute when there are questions about health and functioning. The child’s own account is central in all these contexts.

For parentsFor teachers and school communitiesFor doctors and psychologists

For parents

Understanding the child’s needs at home and at school.

As a parent, you may see a child who immerses themselves in their interests, asks many questions or learns quickly in certain areas. The school may describe a student who shows little engagement or cannot demonstrate what they know. These differing accounts are a starting point for exploring the situation together, rather than settling on one explanation in advance.

Consider interests, learning and well-being together

Academic development needs to be considered alongside social and emotional needs. Parents can support curiosity while also allowing space for friendship, play and leisure. The aim is to understand what the child needs, rather than turning their potential into a demand for high achievement.

Talk with the child about what they enjoy learning, what feels too easy or difficult, and when they feel part of a community. Some children miss academic challenges; others particularly want to find people who share their interests. Needs can change over time.

Support an early interest in reading

When a child shows an interest in letters or can already read, adults can provide books and conversations suited to their understanding and interests. Reading can sit alongside play and time with others. The child does not need to be held back to follow the same pace as their peers.

When the child starts school, share what they enjoy reading, what they understand and the questions that texts prompt. This gives the teacher a starting point for further learning.

Make collaboration with the school specific

A practical starting point for a conversation with the school is to gather examples from everyday life and let the child contribute. Describe situations rather than drawing conclusions about particular traits or causes.

  • Which activities engage the child, and what do they learn through them?
  • What does the child say about tasks, ways of working and relationships at school?
  • What does the teacher observe, and where do the experiences match or differ?
  • What adaptation could be tried, and when will you review together how it is working?

These questions are suggestions for dialogue, not a checklist for identifying high learning potential. The aim is a shared understanding of learning needs and well-being.

These topics can support dialogue between home, school and student.

For teachers and school communities

Pedagogical differentiation, progression and participation in the community.

High learning potential is not always visible in school achievement. Some students cannot demonstrate what they know in everyday teaching. Examine what the student already knows and what can support further learning.

Explore prior knowledge and learning needs

The Norwegian Directorate for Education and Training describes identification as more than tests and assessments. Consider the student’s experiences, knowledge, skills and development. Include information from parents and others who know the student. High learning potential can also coexist with learning difficulties.

Adapt the challenges, not just the workload

Exploration, project work, depth and feedback can guide further learning. When a student has mastered the content, tasks should provide new understanding. More tasks of the same type do not necessarily represent academic progress.

One teaching example might involve students working on a shared topic while encountering different questions and challenges. One student might explore basic relationships, while another compares explanations, tests a hypothesis or explains which solutions are most appropriate.

Monitor both learning and the experience of inclusion

Assessment should inform the next step. Ask whether the challenge is meaningful and whether support is sufficient. Ask whether there is room for the student’s own ideas. Academic differentiation must also be considered alongside participation and classroom relationships.

  • What has the student learned that they did not know before?
  • Can the student use and develop their own strategies?
  • Are there opportunities for academic collaboration and shared interests?
  • Does the student feel that teaching and the community are working better for them?

This is also work for school leaders and staff: discussing practice, sharing experiences and following up adaptations over time. The Directorate emphasises variation in activities, resources and assessment approaches, and the need for schools to monitor students’ development.

For doctors and psychologists

Learning potential, school experiences and a holistic assessment of functioning.

Knowledge about learning potential can help place a child’s strengths, difficulties and everyday life in context. The educational description must be kept separate from assessment of symptoms and diagnoses.

Take both strengths and difficulties seriously

Strong abilities do not rule out co-occurring difficulties. Abilities can also hide difficulties. Assessment includes developmental history, health, strengths, interests and functioning across settings. See the Directorate of Health’s guideline (in Norwegian).

Boredom, restlessness or low participation do not on their own indicate a diagnosis. Examine the factors that may explain the difficulties. Information about the learning environment is part of the context, not a substitute for clinical assessment.

High learning potential and ADHD can coexist. Better concentration during an interesting task does not by itself establish the cause of difficulties. The Norwegian Directorate of Health describes the need to assess functioning across settings, alternative explanations and co-occurring difficulties (in Norwegian).

Understand variation across situations

The child may function differently at home, at school and in activities. Interpret observations in relation to the situation and day-to-day variation. High ability can coexist with neurodevelopmental disorders. Read about assessment components (in Norwegian).

As part of collaborative reflection, professionals can explore what characterises situations in which the child participates well and what is different when they struggle. The child’s experience of tasks, relationships and belonging provides important information about school life.

Connect understanding with the child’s everyday life

A useful topic for collaboration between the family, school and relevant professionals is how support can address both learning needs and other needs. Who follows up on what, how is the child heard and how are experiences reviewed? These questions can guide collaboration without assuming that all difficulties have the same cause.

This section provides professional background for doctors and psychologists. It does not describe an assessment or treatment service offered by Idsøe Rådgivning.

PROFESSIONAL DEVELOPMENT

Ella’s ideas for sharing knowledge and developing practice

How can knowledge about learning potential inform better practice in early childhood education and schools?

Ella has many ideas about developing knowledge with students and professional communities, and about trying out and improving teaching. The ideas concern early knowledge, professional development, student participation and collaboration between research and practice.

Knowledge from early childhood onwards

Ella highlights the importance of adults learning about high learning potential early in a child’s life. The idea is to help early childhood settings understand differing needs and provide appropriate opportunities for learning and exploration.

More knowledge in professional education

She points to higher education as a setting for giving future professionals more knowledge about these children. Teaching and courses on learning potential and differing needs can provide a foundation for professional practice.

Students as co-developers

One idea is for students themselves to help develop activities or courses for professionals. Their experiences of learning, challenges and belonging can help ensure that adults’ professional development is informed by what students experience and need.

Small teaching pilot projects

Ella describes an interest in trying out differentiation in different subjects and examining the experiences systematically. These trials could help identify teaching approaches that work in the Norwegian context.

A research and resource centre

She also outlines the idea of a centre combining research with support for schools and parents. The proposal connects the development of new knowledge with the needs of those who work with and care for children in everyday life.

This presents professional ideas, rather than projects already under way or services available to book. Which ideas Ella would like to develop further through Idsøe Rådgivning remains to be discussed with her.
