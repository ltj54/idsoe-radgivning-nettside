INCLUSION IN PRACTICE

Academic and social inclusion

Inclusion involves more than a student adapting to existing provision. Teaching also needs to be developed around the student’s needs. Physical presence in a mainstream classroom does not by itself tell us whether the student feels included.

Inclusion also involves contributing academically, experiencing social belonging and being met with understanding. A student may attend lessons without feeling that the tasks are meaningful or that their own perspectives have a place.

Equal worth and different learning needs

Equal worth can be respected through different tasks, approaches and levels of support. When everyone studies the same material at the same pace, some may receive too little challenge and others too little support. A shared classroom community can accommodate different paths to learning.

  • Academically: Can the student learn something new and contribute their knowledge and ideas?
  • Socially: Does the student have someone to work with and share interests with?
  • The student’s experience: Does the student feel seen, listened to and part of the community?

These questions need to be explored with the student. Adults’ assessment of good provision may differ from the student’s experience of it.

Academic level does not describe the whole child

A child working on advanced mathematics may still need support with collaboration, disappointment or starting tasks. Different aspects of development do not necessarily progress at the same pace. Explore each area of need without assigning the child a single overall “mental age”.

Flexible approaches that support belonging

These examples can inform planning with the student. Consider learning needs, relationships and the school’s circumstances when choosing an approach.

Lessons in a subject at a higher level

Learning: The student attends some lessons at a more advanced level.

Belonging: Plan connections with their own class, a welcome in the other group and an adult who follows up.

A guided interest group

Learning: Students investigate a shared question, potentially across year groups.

Belonging: Make room for collaboration and new relationships. Review who can participate and how the group works.

In-depth study within the class

Learning: The student explores more complex questions within the class topic.

Belonging: Connect the work to shared discussions and collaboration so that it does not become an isolated activity.

Agree who will follow up, when to review the approach and how the student’s experience will be heard. Check both academic progress and whether the student feels safe and included.

Udir’s guidance explains conditions for grouping and subject acceleration in Norway. Grouping by academic level is subject to specific restrictions.
